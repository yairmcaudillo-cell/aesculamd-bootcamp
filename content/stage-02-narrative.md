# Stage 02: Be Yourself — Narrative

*Foundations Track — unlocks after Stage 01 (Premed 101)*
*Estimated time: 35–45 minutes · Feeds: your evidence log, your future personal statement*

---

## Why this stage exists, and why it's this early

Most students don't write their "why medicine" story until the summer before they apply — under deadline pressure, years removed from the actual moments that shaped them, reconstructing a narrative instead of remembering one. What comes out is usually competent but generic, because it's being manufactured rather than recalled.

This stage exists to capture your real "why" now, while it's still close and specific — not to produce a finished essay. What you write here won't be your personal statement. It'll be the raw, honest material your future personal statement gets built from, two or three years from now, by you.

---

## Part 1: The difference between a "why medicine" story and a real one

Admissions officers read thousands of personal statements a year, and the ones that stand out aren't the most dramatic — they're the most specific and honest. A generic version sounds like: *"I've always wanted to help people, and my volunteer experience confirmed my passion for medicine."* A real version names an actual moment, an actual person, an actual feeling — and is willing to sit with complexity rather than resolve everything neatly.

Your real "why" is allowed to be: unclear, tied to something difficult, still evolving, or connected to something you haven't told many people. This stage isn't asking you to perform certainty. It's asking you to be accurate.

---

## Part 2: Finding the real material

Answer these as specifically as you can — names, places, moments, not summaries. There's no length requirement; a precise two sentences beats a vague paragraph.

1. Think of a specific moment — not a general period of time — when you were close to illness, injury, caregiving, or a healthcare setting, in any capacity. What actually happened? What did you notice about yourself in that moment?
2. Who is one real person (family member, patient, community member, yourself) whose experience with health or illness has stayed with you? What specifically do you remember?
3. If you're a first-generation student, or the first in your family pursuing this path: what has that role — translating, explaining, navigating systems for others — actually required of you? Be specific about a moment, not just the general fact of the role.
4. What's a moment where your interest in medicine felt uncertain, complicated, or you doubted it? (This matters as much as the moments of clarity — real narratives include doubt.)
5. If you had to explain, to someone who knows you well, *why this and not something else*, in one honest sentence — what would you actually say, not what would sound impressive?

---

### What this looks like in practice (a composite, not a real student)

When Marcus answered prompt 1, his real material wasn't a single dramatic scene — it was noticing, at 16, that he was the only person in the ER waiting room who could translate his grandmother's symptoms for the attending physician, and feeling both useful and terrified in the same five minutes. That's the kind of specific, complicated moment this stage is trying to surface — not a tidy, inspirational one, and not one he'd have remembered this clearly if he'd waited until senior year to write it down.

---

## Part 3: What this connects to

Everything you wrote above becomes tagged entries in your evidence log under **Self-Awareness** and **Ethical Responsibility to Self and Others** — two of the hardest competencies to demonstrate convincingly later, because they require this kind of specific self-reflection, not just activity.

This stage will resurface deliberately: in Stage 08 (Research) and Stage 09 (Leadership), you'll be asked whether what you're experiencing there connects back to what you wrote here — building the actual throughline, over years, instead of retrofitting one at the end.

---

## A note on doubt and complexity

If what you wrote above feels unfinished, contradictory, or uncertain — that's not a problem to fix right now. Real reasons for pursuing medicine are rarely clean, and the students who eventually write the strongest personal statements are usually the ones who let their "why" stay honest and specific through years of revisiting it, rather than locking in a tidy version at 18 and performing it for four years.
