# Stage 04: Strategy — MD, DO, or Both

*Foundations Track — unlocks after Stage 03 (Cost & Access)*
*Estimated time: 30–35 minutes · Sets: how later stages get lightly customized to your path*

---

## Why this stage exists

Most students default into "MD" without ever seriously considering DO — not because they've weighed the two, but because DO is less visible in the culture around premed advice. That's a real gap: DO and MD are both complete, licensed paths to becoming a full physician, with real philosophical and practical differences worth an honest look before you build a school list around only one of them.

This stage won't force you to choose forever. It'll help you make a real, informed first decision — MD-focused, DO-focused, or dual-applying to both — that shapes small, specific parts of your roadmap from here forward (which shadowing to prioritize, which letters to plan for), without duplicating your entire curriculum into separate tracks.

---

## Part 1: What actually differs between MD and DO

**Philosophy of care.** DOs are trained in osteopathic manipulative treatment (OMM) — a hands-on diagnostic and treatment approach — alongside the same core medical curriculum MDs receive, with a historical emphasis on whole-person, primary-care-oriented practice. In practice today, both MDs and DOs can specialize in any field and practice in any care setting; the philosophical emphasis at the training level doesn't restrict your eventual specialty choice.

**Residency and licensing.** MD and DO graduates now train through a single, merged residency accreditation system (ACGME), and DO graduates take the COMLEX board exam (some also take the USMLE) while MD graduates take the USMLE. Both paths lead to a fully licensed practicing physician with equal ability to specialize.

**The numbers, honestly.** DO matriculants have historically had somewhat lower average GPA and MCAT numbers than MD matriculants — but this doesn't mean DO is "easier." DO school acceptance rates aren't necessarily higher than MD acceptance rates, and while osteopathic admission requirements are somewhat more inclusive, getting into a DO school isn't easier than getting into an MD program. There are far fewer accredited DO programs than MD programs nationally, which keeps real competition high even with different average stats. If you're aiming for DO, expect competitive GPA/MCAT benchmarks of their own, not a guaranteed easier path.

**Applications are separate systems.** MD applicants apply through AMCAS; DO applicants apply through AACOMAS — different platforms, different fees, different (though overlapping) timelines. Dual-applying means managing both, which has real cost and time implications — the ones you just saw broken out in Cost & Access.

**Letters of recommendation.** Most DO programs require at least one letter from a physician (MD or DO) — and many specifically prefer or strongly recommend that it come from a DO, though this varies school by school and only a few make it an outright requirement. Either way, it's a logistical detail worth knowing now: a DO-leaning student benefits from seeking out DO shadowing or mentorship early enough to have that relationship in place well before application season, rather than scrambling for a DO letter the summer before applying.

**State residency changes the math more than almost anything else in this stage.** For public MD and DO schools, in-state applicants are frequently admitted at several times the rate out-of-state applicants are — some public schools accept only a small handful of non-residents a year, regardless of stats. Before you build any mental model of reach/target/safety, years from now, know your own state's public-school landscape now — it's one of the most under-discussed facts in premed strategy, and it changes how competitive a given GPA/MCAT combination actually is for you specifically.

## Part 2: Reflection — matching the path to you, not to prestige assumptions

1. When you picture the kind of medicine you want to practice — hands-on, whole-person, primary-care-adjacent, or highly specialized and procedural — which philosophy, as described above, resonates more, or does it feel like it doesn't matter much to you either way?
2. Have you had any exposure to an osteopathic physician or OMM specifically? If not, is that something worth deliberately seeking out before you decide?
3. Are you drawn to DO, MD, or dual-applying for reasons connected to your own values and interests — or mostly because of assumptions about prestige or difficulty? Be honest; this isn't a graded answer, it's for you.
4. Given what you saw in Cost & Access about separate applications, separate fees, and the letter-of-recommendation difference — does dual-applying feel realistic for your time and budget, or does focusing on one path make more sense right now?
5. Look up your home state's public medical school(s) and their out-of-state acceptance rate. Does what you find change how you're thinking about any of this?

### What this looks like in practice (a composite, not a real student)

Jamal assumed he'd apply MD-only until a shadowing shift with a DO sports-medicine physician showed him OMM being used on an athlete he'd watched get injured the week before — the whole-person framing clicked in a way lecture-hall descriptions hadn't. He didn't abandon MD; he decided to dual-apply, and used what he'd just seen in Cost & Access to budget for two application fees instead of assuming he'd sort that out later. He also started asking that DO physician for shadowing time specifically with an eventual letter in mind, rather than waiting until junior year to think about who his DO letter-writer would be.

## Part 3: What this decision actually changes downstream

Choosing MD-focused, DO-focused, or dual-apply doesn't fork you into a separate curriculum — it lightly tags a few things in your roadmap:
- **Clinical Experience** stage will surface DO-shadowing opportunities specifically if you're DO-leaning or dual-applying
- **Personal Brand / LOR** stage will flag the osteopathic-letter requirement early enough to act on it
- Since you already saw AMCAS vs. AACOMAS's real, separate fee structures back in Cost & Access, dual-applying is budgeted rather than a mid-cycle surprise

This choice isn't permanent — you can revisit it any time, and revisiting it doesn't erase anything in your evidence log, only updates which tags get surfaced going forward.
