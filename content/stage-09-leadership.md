# Stage 09: Leadership

*Foundations Track — unlocks after Stage 08 (Research or No Research?)*
*Estimated time: 25–30 minutes · Unlocks: nothing new — strengthens your evidence log directly*

---

## Why this stage exists

"Get a leadership position" is advice that sends a lot of premeds chasing titles — club president, team captain, chapter founder — without much thought for whether they actually did anything once they had the title. Admissions committees see through this quickly. A student who became president of an organization and changed nothing reads very differently from a student who took on a much smaller, unofficial role and clearly made something happen.

This stage exists to help you find or build a real leadership experience — one with actual substance — rather than collect a title for its own sake.

---

## Part 1: What "leadership" actually means here

Leadership, in the way admissions committees read it, has almost nothing to do with a title and everything to do with three things:

- **Initiative** — did you notice a real problem or gap and do something about it, rather than waiting to be assigned a role that already existed?
- **Responsibility for others** — did people depend on you, and did you follow through?
- **Working through other people** — did you motivate, coordinate, or bring out the best in a group, rather than just doing good individual work?

None of this requires a medical context. A founding role in a campus organization, informal peer tutoring you took the initiative to organize, training new volunteers at your clinic, coordinating a team project, coaching — all of it counts, as long as you can point to something specific you actually did, not just a position you held.

**The honest trap to avoid:** joining multiple organizations specifically to angle for a title, then holding that title without real substance behind it. Admissions committees read hundreds of applications a year and can usually tell the difference between someone who led and someone who merely occupied a position. A smaller role done with real initiative reads as stronger evidence than a big title held passively.

---

## Part 2: What a realistic target looks like

There's no official requirement, but a reasonable planning target is **three or more distinct leadership experiences, each sustained for at least a few months, across your undergraduate years** — not a dozen shallow one-off roles. Depth and follow-through matter far more than the count.

**The strongest leadership stories tend to follow one pattern:** identifying a problem nobody else was solving, and doing something about it — rather than simply inheriting a role someone else already built. Founding or building something is harder and slower than stepping into an existing structure, but it's also the version that teaches you the most and reads as the most genuine.

---

### What this looks like in practice (a composite, not a real student)

Fatima noticed new volunteers at her free clinic kept making the same intake-paperwork mistakes because no one trained them consistently — so she wrote up a one-page onboarding guide and started walking new volunteers through it herself, without being asked or given a title for it. A year later the clinic coordinator formalized it as an official role. The strength of the story isn't the eventual title; it's that she solved a real, specific problem before anyone gave her permission to.

---

## Part 3: Reflection

1. Think of a specific moment where you took initiative on something — not because you were assigned to, but because you noticed it needed doing. What happened?
2. Is there a real, current gap you've noticed — in a club, your clinic, your community, anywhere — that nobody else seems to be solving? Would you actually want to be the one to solve it, or is this stage surfacing something you're not genuinely drawn to yet?
3. If you're currently holding any titled positions, be honest: are you actively doing something with that role, or holding it passively? What's one thing you could change about how you're showing up in it?
4. Have you ever been responsible for training, mentoring, or coordinating other people specifically? What did you learn about yourself in that role?
